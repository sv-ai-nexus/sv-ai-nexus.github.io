# Invitation

AI is already drafting contracts, reviewing documents, and flagging legal risk. Law firms and in-house teams are adopting it, investors are pricing it into deals, and it is showing up in court filings. But when AI does work that once required a lawyer's or a judge's judgment, <span class="emph">who is accountable?</span>

For our **<span style="color: red;">5th Chapter</span>** we put three people on one stage who see that question from three different seats: the engineer building legal AI, the deal lawyer vetting AI companies, and the judge who decides what happens when AI-assisted work reaches the courtroom. Light dinner first, generously sponsored by **KIC Silicon Valley**, then three talks, one panel, and extended networking.

# Talk Details

## Talk 1 &ndash; Legal AI in 2026: Who's Adopting It, How, and What's Next {#talk-1}

<div class="lf-speaker lf-reveal">
<div class="lf-icon">⌨️</div>
<div>
<p class="lf-name"><a target="_blank" href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/ann-kang/">Eunwon (Ann) Kang</a></p>
<p class="lf-aff">Legal Engineer @ Harvey</p>
</div>
</div>

<p class="lf-thesis">Legal AI has moved from pilot to daily tool, but adoption looks very different from the headlines.</p>

From inside Harvey, one of the leading legal AI companies, this talk gives a ground-level view of who is using AI for legal work in the US and Korea, and what that use actually looks like.

This talk will cover:

- How many law firms and in-house teams in the US and Korea are adopting AI.
- What AI adoption actually looks like day to day.
- Harvey's mission and business model.
- Where Harvey and other legal AI companies are growing next.

## Talk 2 &ndash; Inside AI Startup Deals: What's Getting Funded, What's Getting Bought, and What Gets Checked {#talk-2}

<div class="lf-speaker lf-reveal">
<div class="lf-icon">🤝</div>
<div>
<p class="lf-name"><a target="_blank" href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/catharina-min-b2b6515/">Catharina Min</a></p>
<p class="lf-aff">Partner @ Covington &amp; Burling</p>
</div>
</div>

<p class="lf-thesis">When the target is an AI company, diligence asks questions that did not exist a few years ago.</p>

A Silicon Valley deal lawyer walks through what venture and M&A activity in AI looks like right now, and what investors and acquirers look at before they sign.

This talk will cover:

- Current VC and M&A trends in AI, including new deal structures.
- What investors and acquirers scrutinize: data rights, IP ownership, model licenses, and regulatory exposure.
- What founders should get right before raising or selling.

## Talk 3 &ndash; When AI Reaches the Courtroom: AI-Assisted Filings and the Court's Own Use of AI {#talk-3}

<div class="lf-speaker lf-reveal">
<div class="lf-icon">🏛️</div>
<div>
<p class="lf-name">Ji Yoon Han</p>
<p class="lf-aff">Judge @ Daejeon District Court (former member of the Judicial AI Research Society)</p>
</div>
</div>

<p class="lf-thesis">AI-assisted work is now arriving at the bench, and courts are beginning to use AI themselves.</p>

Judge Han holds a B.S. in Computer Science and an M.S. in Computer Engineering, served as a law clerk at the Seoul High Court and the Seoul Central District Court from 2012, practiced as an attorney, and was appointed to the bench in 2017. She has handled patent, trademark, and copyright disputes in the Intellectual Property Division of the Seoul Central District Court and at the Patent Court of Korea, reviewed the Korea Copyright Commission's 2023 Guide to Copyright Issues Related to Generative AI, and served on its 2024 AI and Copyright Policy Reform Working Group.

This talk will cover:

- The problems courts are seeing in AI-assisted filings.
- How courts are starting to use AI themselves.

## Panel Discussion &ndash; Who Signs Off? Builders, Deal Lawyers, and the Bench {#panel-discussion}

<div class="lf-speaker lf-reveal">

# Why This Forum Matters

AI is entering legal work from both ends: tools that do the work, and rules that govern the tools. In between sit founders shipping products, investors pricing them, and lawyers and judges asked to sign off on output they did not write. The gap between what AI can do and what someone is willing to be accountable for is where most of the risk now lives.

Three questions define this moment:

1. **Who is accountable when AI does legal work?**
   Professional responsibility and liability rules were written for humans using tools, not for tools that do the human's work.

1. **What do investors and acquirers now check in an AI company?**
   Data rights, IP ownership, model licenses, and regulatory exposure have become deal terms, not footnotes.

1. **What happens when AI reaches the courtroom?**
   Courts are seeing AI-assisted filings and starting to use AI themselves, and Korea and the US are working out the rules at the same time.
